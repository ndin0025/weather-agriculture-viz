Climate is one of the most important drivers of Australian agriculture, influencing water availability, production conditions, and farming risk. However, favourable weather does not always guarantee stronger economic outcomes. While rainfall and temperature shape agricultural conditions across the country, differences in farm income and climate-related costs reveal a more complex relationship. This dashboard explores how environmental conditions, agricultural profitability, and financial risk interact across Australian states between 2010 and 2017.

01

Australia's climate is highly uneven

Rainfall, temperature, and farm income vary sharply across states. The maps provide the geographic overview before looking at time trends and costs.

Income insight

Farm income is concentrated in several major agricultural states, particularly Queensland, Victoria and New South Wales. These states benefit from relatively favourable climate conditions, combining moderate rainfall with suitable temperatures for agricultural production. This supports stronger agricultural activity and comparatively high income across Australia states.

Temperature insight

Temperature differences across Australia create very different agricultural conditions between states. Hotter regions such as the Northern Territory and inland Queensland often require greater irrigation and face higher risks of drought and heat stress, while cooler states such as Tasmania and Victoria are more suitable for dairy farming, livestock production, and temperature-sensitive crops.

Rainfall insight

Tasmania records the highest average rainfall, while South Australia and parts of the inland west remain much drier. States along the east coast, particularly Queensland, New South Wales, and Victoria, benefit from more stable rainfall patterns that support cropping, dairy farming, and livestock production. In contrast, drier regions often rely heavily on irrigation and are more vulnerable to drought, water shortages, and lower agricultural reliability.

02

Weather patterns shape farming conditions

Rainfall is not just about yearly totals. Timing, seasonal pattern, and wind conditions also affect farming risk.

Rainfall intensity insight

Tasmania consistently records the highest rainfall intensity throughout the entire period, with rainfall levels remaining substantially above all other states. In contrast, South Australia and Western Australia experience the lowest rainfall intensity and show relatively little variation over time. Queensland and Victoria maintain moderate-to-high rainfall levels, while most states exhibit a noticeable decline around 2014 before recovering in subsequent years. This suggests that climatic conditions are relatively stable over time and continue to shape agricultural water availability across regions. States with higher rainfall benefit from more reliable water resources for farming, while drier states face greater dependence on irrigation and are more exposed to water scarcity and drought-related challenges.

Rainfall by state insight

Tasmania records by far the highest average rainfall among all Australian states, receiving more than twice the rainfall of most mainland regions. Victoria and Queensland also experience relatively high rainfall levels, while South Australia and Western Australia remain considerably drier. This substantial variation highlights the uneven distribution of water resources across Australia, creating different agricultural conditions and levels of dependence on irrigation. States with lower rainfall are likely to face greater challenges related to water scarcity and drought risk.

Wind insight

The wind rose indicates that several wind directions occur more frequently than others, with easterly and northerly-related directions appearing particularly dominant. This suggests that wind patterns are not evenly distributed and may influence local farming conditions differently across regions. Frequent strong winds can increase evaporation rates, reduce soil moisture, and place additional stress on crops, especially in areas that already experience limited rainfall.

03

Climate advantage does not guarantee profitability

Farm income and production costs move differently across states. Use the dropdowns in the charts to focus on one state.

Income trend insight

Farm income generally increased across most states between 2010 and 2017, although growth rates varied considerably. Queensland and Victoria consistently recorded the highest income levels and experienced the strongest long-term growth, while Northern Territory and Tasmania remained among the lowest-income regions throughout the period. Several states also experienced short-term fluctuations, particularly around 2014-2015, suggesting that farm profitability is influenced not only by climate conditions but also by market demand, production scale, commodity prices, and management practices.

Cost pressure insight

Cost composition is remarkably similar across all states, with fertiliser accounting for the largest share of agricultural expenses, followed by labour and insurance costs. The relatively consistent cost structure suggests that farmers across Australia face comparable input requirements regardless of location. However, because these costs remain substantial even in high-income states, favourable climate conditions do not automatically translate into higher profitability. Instead, farm performance depends on the ability to manage input costs while maintaining productivity and revenue growth.

04

Climate risk is becoming a financial burden

The scatterplots test whether more rainfall or higher temperature clearly explains farm income and insurance pressure.

Rainfall and income insight

More rainfall does not necessarily lead to higher farm income. Tasmania records the highest rainfall levels but does not achieve the highest income, while Queensland and Victoria generate stronger income with more moderate rainfall. The slight negative trend line further suggests that higher rainfall alone does not guarantee stronger financial performance. This indicates that agricultural profitability depends on a combination of factors including production scale, crop mix, market conditions, and farm management rather than rainfall availability alone.

Heat and insurance insight

The relationship between temperature and insurance costs appears weak and varies considerably across states. Some hotter states experience relatively low insurance costs, while others face substantially higher costs despite similar temperature levels. The scattered distribution of observations and the nearly flat trend line suggest that temperature alone is not a strong predictor of insurance expenses. Instead, insurance costs are likely influenced by a broader range of climate-related risks, including drought exposure, extreme weather events, and regional vulnerability.
