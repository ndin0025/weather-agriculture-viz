Climate is one of the most important drivers of Australian agriculture, influencing water availability, production conditions, and farming risk. However, favourable weather does not always guarantee stronger economic outcomes. While rainfall and temperature shape agricultural conditions across the country, differences in farm income and climate-related costs reveal a more complex relationship. This dashboard explores how environmental conditions, agricultural profitability, and financial risk interact across Australian states between 2010 and 2017.

01

Australia's climate is highly uneven

Rainfall, temperature, and farm income vary sharply across states. The maps provide the geographic overview before looking at time trends and costs.

Income insight

Farm income is concentrated in several major agricultural states, particularly Queensland, Victoria and New South Wales. These states benefit from relatively favourable climate conditions, combining moderate rainfall with suitable temperatures for agricultural production. This supports stronger agricultural activity and comparatively high income across Australia states.

Temperature insight

Temperature differences across Australia create very different agricultural conditions between states. Hotter regions such as the Northern Territory and inland Queensland often require greater irrigation and face higher risks of drought and heat stress, while cooler states such as Tasmania and Victoria are more suitable for dairy farming, livestock production, and temperature-sensitive crops.

Rainfall insight

Tasmania records the highest average rainfall, while South Australia and parts of the inland west remain much drier. States along the east coast, particularly Queensland, New South Wales, and Victoria, benefit from more stable rainfall patterns that support cropping, dairy farming, and livestock production. In contrast, drier regions often rely heavily on irrigation and are more vulnerable to drought, water shortages, and lower agricultural reliability.

02

Weather patterns shape farming conditions

Rainfall is not just about yearly totals. Timing, seasonal pattern, and wind conditions also affect farming risk.

Rainfall intensity insight

Rainfall intensity changes across both states and years, showing that farming risk depends on more than average rainfall totals. Tasmania remains consistently wetter, while drier states such as South Australia and Western Australia show lower rainfall intensity. This highlights how seasonal rainfall patterns can affect water availability, crop planning, and drought exposure.

Rainfall by state insight

Tasmania records the highest average rainfall, while South Australia and Western Australia remain much drier. This uneven rainfall distribution means some states have stronger natural water availability, while others rely more heavily on irrigation and water management.

Wind insight

Wind conditions add another layer of agricultural risk. Frequent gust patterns can increase evaporation, reduce soil moisture, and make farming conditions less stable, especially when rainfall is already limited.

03

Climate advantage does not guarantee profitability

Farm income and production costs move differently across states. Use the dropdowns in the charts to focus on one state.

Income trend insight

Farm income increases unevenly across states, showing that favourable climate conditions do not automatically lead to stronger profitability. Queensland, Victoria and New South Wales remain among the stronger income states, but income patterns also depend on production scale, market conditions, and farm management rather than rainfall or temperature alone.

Cost pressure insight

Cost composition is relatively similar across states, with fertiliser making up the largest share of agricultural costs. This suggests that even states with strong climate advantages still face major input costs, which can reduce the financial benefits of favourable rainfall or temperature conditions.

04

Climate risk is becoming a financial burden

The scatterplots test whether more rainfall or higher temperature clearly explains farm income and insurance pressure.

Rainfall and income insight

More rainfall does not always mean higher farm income. High-rainfall states such as Tasmania do not necessarily achieve the highest income levels, while some moderate-rainfall states perform strongly. This shows that profitability depends on wider economic, operational, and regional factors, not rainfall alone.

Heat and insurance insight

Higher temperatures are linked with greater climate-related financial pressure in some states. Hotter regions may face higher insurance costs because of increased exposure to drought, heat stress, and extreme weather risk, making climate risk a direct cost for agricultural producers.
